# Part 3: The outcome

Read when Part 3 starts, right after its signpost, and say first: "Next, we'll describe what a good [deliverable] looks like, so every draft can be checked against it." Purpose: a goal statement and its checks, in a form testing can grade. Every quoted line is said exactly; `[deliverable]` is what Part 2 named ("follow-up email"). The confidential check and the Sensitivity and Provenance definitions are in SKILL.md.

## 1. A real example first

Before question 1, say once: "Whenever I ask for an example, an output or an input, don't paste real client names, personal details or anything confidential. Swap them for made-up ones, or tell me what it looks like and I'll draft a made-up example. What matters is that it looks like your real work."

**Ask:** "Do you have a recent [deliverable] you were happy with — one you'd point to and say 'exactly like that'?"
- *Why:* When we test the workflow, its first drafts are compared against it.
- *Not sure?* Say no for now. If you have one, paste it with names and companies swapped for made-up ones; the wording and structure are what count.

On a yes, ask for it (paste or attach; a Context Inventory ID is also fine) and read it before asking anything else. Save a pasted one as `framework/[name]/context/C<n>-<short-name>.md`, set its Sensitivity and Provenance, run the confidential check if it applies, and record it in the Context Inventory with that path as its location. It becomes the **Golden Example**: only a real output can be one (a past output with its details changed still counts).

After a yes, in its own message, **Ask:** "What did you start from for that one?" That input becomes the first `(real)` scenario, E1, and holds the Golden Example in its row.

On a no: no Golden Example. The `Reference example:` line and the Golden Example column hold "—". Don't ask what they started from.

## The recipe

Say: "We'll describe a good [deliverable] in three steps: say it, sharpen it, then make it checkable."

## 2. Say it

**Ask:** "Imagine it's built and working. What does a good [deliverable] look like?"
- *Why:* Your answer becomes the target for every draft, and the list we write next is how each draft gets graded.
- *Example:* One page, in plain words, listing every open item with an owner and a date.
- *Not sure?* Tell me what a bad one gets wrong, and I'll work from that.

Only when Parts 1–2 haven't said who receives it (rare: Part 2's description names who receives or acts on it), in its own message, **Ask:** "Once it's ready, who gets it?" Its answer fills the goal's [who] blank; anything else goes in Value & Measurement's Desired Outcome.

## 3. Sharpen it

Fill each blank of the goal sentence from every answer so far (Parts 1–2, "say it", the Analyze card). [trigger] comes from Part 2; [who] from Part 2 or the question above. Ask only for blanks no answer fills, one per message, each just the question: "How long should the [deliverable] be — a few lines, or a full page?" ([format or length]), "When does it need to be ready?" ([when]).

The goal sentence merges the deliverable and its format into one phrase, names the reader as [who], and puts any review step with "ready":

*For each [trigger], a [format] [deliverable] to [who], ready for you to [review] [when].*

("For each scoping call that ends, a short, easy-to-skim follow-up email to the prospect, a few paragraphs long, ready for you to review and send the same day.") The goal keeps the student's own words; numbers live only in the list.

Then, in its own message, **Ask:** "Here's your goal: [sentence]. What would you change or clarify?" and add, after the question mark, "(The [parts they named in 'say it'] become the list we write next.)"

The goal sentence has no "containing [required parts]" blank: the required parts live only in the list, so testing grades each part once. Never invent a sample output for the student to react to; questions sharpen it.

## 4. Make it checkable

**Ask:** "You said a good one has [parts from 'say it']. What else would you check before you send it?" When the student named no parts: **Ask:** "When a drafted [deliverable] lands in front of you, what would you check before you send it?"
- *Why:* Each point you'd check becomes a yes-or-no check on every draft.
- *Example:* a finance analyst might say: "Every number ties to the ledger, and any change over 5% has a reason."

Then gather every vague word left in the answers so far (*good, engaging, thorough, clear, professional, short, easy to skim, key points* and the like) into one message, each with a concrete version tagged *(my suggestion)*: "A few of your words need pinning down so anyone could check them. Keep or change each: '[word]' → [concrete version] *(my suggestion)* …", ending "What would you change or add?" A concrete version the student already gave (a number, a length) is used as theirs, tagged *(you said)*. A follow-up you write here asks what a phrase means, never which miss would make them send it back: that is section 5's question, asked as written even when an earlier answer already named one.

Each answer becomes a yes/no check, kept only if someone else could check it, yes or no, without asking the student. Never record a scale word ("mostly", "somewhat", "1–5"). Play the list back numbered, in plain words, opening "Here's what every [deliverable] must get right:" and ending "What would you change or add?"

## 5. The send-back test

Picking from the list just written:

**Ask:** "Which of these could you never let go out wrong — the ones where a single miss sends it back?"
- *Why:* Every draft is checked against all of these. Missing one you can't live without always sends it back; any other miss, you decide whether to fix or accept.

After the answer, say: "That makes '[item]' a must-have: a miss always sends it back." (for several, name each). These become the `(must)` checks, at most two.

A prohibition the student volunteers here ("never names a client before they've approved it") becomes a Must never rule (an R row), not a check; must-haves are qualities of the output.

Then play the full goal back once more, nothing removed, in its own message: **Ask:** "Here's your full goal: [sentence]. What would you change or clarify?"

**Recorded now:** the Goal and the Acceptance Criteria (AC1…, at most two marked (must)); the real example, if any, in the Context Inventory, and E1 if they gave its input.
