# Part 3: The outcome

Read when Part 3 starts, right after its signpost. Purpose: a goal statement and its checks, in a form Test can grade. Every quoted line is said exactly. The confidential check and the Sensitivity and Provenance definitions are in SKILL.md.

## 1. A real example first

Before question 1, say once: "Whenever I ask for an example, an output, or an input, don't paste real client names, personal details, or anything confidential. Make the details up, or tell me what it looks like and I'll draft a realistic stand-in. What matters is that it looks like your real work."

**Ask:** "Do you have a recent output of this work that you were happy with — one you'd point to and say 'exactly like that'?"
- *Why:* Test compares its first runs against a real output you were happy with.
- *Not sure?* Say no for now. You can add one later.

On a yes, ask for it (paste or attach; a Context Inventory ID is also fine) and read it before asking anything else. Save a pasted one as `framework/[name]/context/C<n>-<short-name>.md`, set its Sensitivity and Provenance, run the confidential check if it applies, and record it in the Context Inventory with that path as its location. It becomes the **Golden Example**: only a real output can be one (a past output with its details changed still counts).

After a yes, in its own message, **Ask:** "What did you start from for that one?" That input becomes the first `(real)` scenario, E1, and holds the Golden Example in its row.

On a no: no Golden Example. The `Reference example:` line and the Golden Example column hold "—". Don't ask what they started from.

## The recipe

Say: "We'll shape this in three steps: say it, sharpen it, make it checkable."

## 2. Say it

**Ask:** "You said it produces [deliverable]. Imagine it's built and working. What does a good one look like?"
- *Why:* Your answer becomes the goal; the checks we write next are what Test grades.

Then, in its own message, **Ask:** "And what do you do with it next?" Its answer fills the goal's [who] blank; anything else goes in Value & Measurement's Desired Outcome.

## 3. Sharpen it

Fill each blank of the goal sentence from the answers and the Analyze card:

*For each [trigger], a [deliverable] for [who], in [format or length], ready [when].*

[trigger] comes from Part 2. Ask only for blanks you can't fill, one per message, each just the question: "Who is it for?" ([who]), "What form should it take, and how long?" ([format or length]), "When does it need to be ready?" ([when]).

Then, in its own message, **Ask:** "Here's your goal: [sentence]. What's wrong?"

The goal sentence has no "containing [required parts]" blank: the required parts live only in the checks, so Test grades each part once and the goal and checks can't drift apart. Never invent a sample output for the student to react to; questions sharpen it.

## 4. Make it checkable

**Ask:** "If the AI handed you one of these, could you say done or not done just by looking? What would you look for?"
- *Why:* Each thing you'd look for becomes a check Test grades yes or no.
- *Example:* a finance analyst might say: "Every number ties to the ledger, and any change over 5% has a reason."

Then flag any vague words left (*good, engaging, thorough, clear, professional*) and ask, one at a time: "What would that look like in the result?" A follow-up you write here asks what the phrase means, never which miss would make them send it back: that is section 5's question, asked as written even when an earlier answer already named one.

Each answer becomes a yes/no check, kept only if someone else could check it, yes or no, without asking the student. Never record a scale word ("mostly", "somewhat", "1–5"). Play the checks back as a numbered list in plain words and ask: "Is that the list? What's missing?"

## 5. The send-back test

Picking from the checks just written:

**Ask:** "If it got everything else right but missed one of these, which one would make you send it back?"
- *Why:* Those become the one or two **(must)** checks. One miss on a (must) check fails that test case.

A prohibition the student volunteers here ("never names a client before they've approved it") becomes a Must never rule (an R row), not a check; (must) checks are for qualities of the output.

Then play the goal back once more, with any blank a check now covers removed (a "fits on one page" check removes [format or length]), in its own message: **Ask:** "Here's your goal now: [sentence]. What's wrong?"

**Recorded now:** the Goal and the Acceptance Criteria (AC1…, at most two marked (must)); the real example, if any, in the Context Inventory, and E1 if they gave its input.
